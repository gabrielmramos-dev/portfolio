As an educator and instructor, I spend a good part of my days trying to make complex technical concepts make sense to people who are just starting out. Over time, I realized that this effort to teach is not just an educational "soft skill"; it is a technical skill in disguise that changed how I design systems and write code.

## Studying is not accumulating information, it is organizing understanding

Many developers confuse studying with consuming content. Teaching showed me that truly learning takes mental organization. Today, when I study a new technology, as I recently did while implementing messaging flows with **RabbitMQ,** I keep asking myself one question: *"How would I explain this to someone else?"*. That shift in perspective makes sure I understand the fundamentals before trying to apply the solution.

## Clear code is a consequence of teaching skills

When you try to explain something you don't understand well, the inconsistencies show up fast. In development, I brought that criterion into my day to day: if I can't explain what a piece of code does in a few sentences, it is probably badly written.

That constant exposure trained me to look for clarity before execution. In practice, it results in:

- **Smaller, focused functions**: each block of code has a single clear responsibility.

- **Semantic naming**: variables and methods that explain their own existence.

- **Less technical debt**: hacks become hard to justify when you have to "explain" the system.

## Breaking problems down: the skill teachers and devs share

A good class starts with the foundation, not with the technical detail. Teaching logic and algorithms forced me to break big problems into understandable parts. When designing **Node.js** architectures with **Clean Architecture**, I started thinking like an instructor: what is this module's real responsibility? What happens if someone changes it without understanding the whole?

## Technical communication as part of engineering

A large share of the problems in software projects does not come from bad code, but from bad communication. Having to adjust my language every day for different kinds of students made me a better collaborator in technical teams. Pull requests get clearer, documentation gets more objective and technical decisions become easier to justify to the team or to the business.

## Conclusion

Working on my teaching skills did not take me away from software development. On the contrary, they taught me to think better before coding. If you want to grow as a developer, learn to explain what you already know. Teaching is one of the fastest, and most neglected, ways to become a better programmer.
